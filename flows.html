Tano · Flows

Pipeline

How a creator goes from first found to searchable: five services, joined by SQS queues.

What happens

1ECS FARGATE

Discover creators

Runs on a timer or when someone asks. Finds creators by hashtag search, similar accounts, provider lists or a manual seed, using the Instagram API and a third-party API. Checks each one, removes duplicates and saves it. Then sends creator.discovered for each new creator.

2LAMBDA

Get the profile

Takes each creator event and reads the profile from the Instagram API. A second timer asks for refreshes. Saves the profile and sends profile.ingested.

3ECS FARGATE

Get the content

Downloads the creator’s posts. Saves the post data in the database and the raw videos and photos in S3.

4

Extract facts

Reads the raw videos and photos and asks the LLM to pull out facts. Saves the facts, then sends an event.

5

Make it searchable

Copies each creator into the OpenSearch cluster. Users search there.

Brand programme

How a brand gets a shortlist of creators, approves it, and starts talking to them.

Inside Creator Scoring
  1. Filter: keep only creators in the right country, language and follower range.
  2. Search: find creators who match the brief, by words and by meaning, and keep the top 2,000.
  3. Check rules: remove anyone who breaks a rule, for example working with a competitor.
  4. Score: give three marks, Fit, Post and Sell. All three must be good.
  5. Shortlist: keep the best, each with a short reason. The brand can approve the list.

What happens

1ECS FARGATE

Set up the programme

The brand sends its programme to the Brand Programme API. The API saves it in the database and puts a job on the scoring queue.

2ECS FARGATE

Score creators

Creator Scoring takes the job, searches OpenSearch and builds a shortlist in five steps: filter, search, check rules, score, shortlist. When it is done, it sends the shortlist back to the API.

3

Reach out

The brand approves the shortlist. Each approved creator goes on the outreach queue, and one Outreach Agent per creator talks to them. Each conversation is saved in outreach_threads.

After the gift arrives, Post Tracker checks the creator’s new posts every 5 minutes for 30 days.

Tano learns: the Experiment Engine

It keeps the score of every test and decides how many new creators get each option.

What happens

1

Wake up

The Experiment Engine runs at every sale (Attribution tells it) and every hour, to pick up new posts and replies.

2ECS FARGATE

Read the score

It reads, for each option in each test, how many creators replied, posted and sold.

3

Save the split

It works out how sure it is about each option and saves the new split, like 80% / 10% / 10%. The better an option does, the more new creators get it.

4

Use the split

The Outreach Agent reads the split to choose what the next creator gets, for example which message or which reward.

5

The brand sees the scoreboard

The brand team sees the scoreboards below: which option leads, how sure Tano is, and who gets what next.

What each one tells the Experiment Engine

  • Reply (comes in days): did the message work? It’s the score for the “What to say?” test.
  • Post (comes in weeks): is the creator actually doing something with the gift? It’s an early warning, because no post means no sales.
  • Sale (comes in 30–60 days): did it really make money? It’s the score for the “Who to recruit?” and “Which reward?” tests.

Why the engine needs the fast ones: with only sales, it would wait a month or two before it could change anything. With replies and posts, it can start sending more creators to the better options within days.

Relationship Agent

What happens after the first post. The Relationship Agent moves each creator up a ladder, from a one-off affiliate to a brand ambassador, over months, with no one watching.

What happens at each review

This diagram shows one review of one creator. The example is Studio Vega on 12 Dec. Studio Vega moves up from Affiliate to Regular and gets a gift, the Dahlia Bunch. The shaded strip in each box shows the example. The boxes on the left show what happens when the answer is yes. On the right is the database. An arrow out of it is a read. An arrow into it is a write.

1

Who is due?

Every hour the Relationship Planner asks the database which creators need a look: their review date has come, or they have a new post or sale. It puts one message per creator on the queue.

2

Read the facts

The Relationship Agent reads the creator’s step on the ladder, posts, sales, replies, the last email and the brand’s settings.

3

Decide the next step

The brand’s settings decide: move up, stay, check in, pause or end. Then the limits: not too many emails, enough budget, nothing paused. Too big or unclear: ask the brand.

4

Act

Claude writes a personal email and SES sends it. Or Commerce Connector orders the next gift from the shop. Or the brand gets one question to answer.

5

Save it and set the next date

The decision, its reason and the new step are saved. The next review date is set, for example in 30 days. Then the creator is not looked at again until that date, or until a new post or sale.

Why it can run for months alone

It remembers nothing in its head

Each creator’s step and next date are rows in the database. A crash or a new release loses nothing: the Planner simply picks the creator again.

Every action happens once

Each email or gift gets a unique key before it is sent. If something is retried, the key is already there, so nothing is sent twice.

Rules decide, Claude writes

Moving up, gifts and commission follow the brand’s settings, so they are predictable. Claude only writes emails and reads replies.

Limits on everything

At most one email per creator every 14 days, a gift budget per month, a top commission. Anything above a limit waits for the brand.

A watchdog

Every hour it checks bounces, complaints, spend and errors. If something looks wrong, it pauses the agent for that brand and alerts Tano.

People see only the hard cases

An unclear reply, money above a limit, a complaint. Everything else runs alone, and every decision is saved with its reason.

What no brand can change

  • A creator who says stop never gets another email.
  • #ad is always required.
  • Claude never decides money. Steps, gifts and commission come only from the settings.
  • Never more than one email a week, and never more than 3 check-ins, whatever the settings say.
  • Nothing is ever sent twice.
  • The Watchdog can always pause the agent.

When a brand changes a setting

  1. The change is saved as a new version. Older versions are kept.
  2. Nothing is sent straight away. Each creator’s next review uses the new version.
  3. Creators keep the commission they already have until their next step change, so nobody gets a surprise cut.
  4. Every decision saves the version it used, so you can always see why the agent did something.

Why it is built this way

  • One generic model. A creator, a post, a brand and a city are all rows in entities, so any of them can have facts about any other.
  • One writer. Only Enrich writes the graph. A creator’s or a post’s entity points at the business row through creator_id or content_id; the business tables, written by Ingest, do not point back.
  • The ontology is the contract with the AI. predicates and concepts say which relationships exist and which types they connect. They are loaded by database migrations.
  • Evidence makes results explainable. We can always answer “why does Tano think this?”.
  • Every AI call is recorded with model, prompt version and cost. Changing a prompt never deletes history; newer facts supersede older ones.
  • Why not a graph database? The workload is transactional writes with uniqueness rules, which PostgreSQL does well in one store. A graph projection can be added later for multi-hop queries.

OpenSearch models

Three indexes, filled by step 4 · Index from PostgreSQL. Each one is reached through an alias (entities, creators, content), so a mapping change means building a v2 index and switching the alias. Every document carries pg_version, so a late or repeated write never replaces newer data. Press See example on an index to see one of its documents. Below the indexes: a hybrid search example.

Lookup and type-ahead over brands, places, products, topics and people so the UI can turn free text into entity filters and the query planner can validate entity names.

11 fields · 1 primary × 2 replicas · refresh 10s

  • entity_id_idkeyword
    Document _id. Merged entities are deleted from the index; the survivor keeps the id.
    PostgreSQL: entities.id
  • entity_typekeyword
    brand | place | product | topic | person | organisation | event. The entities of creators and posts (entities.creator_id or entities.content_id set) are not indexed here: they are part of the creators-v1 and content-v1 documents.
    PostgreSQL: entity_types.name via entities.entity_type_id
  • canonical_nametext .sayt .keyword
    Lookup name. The .sayt sub-field materialises _2gram, _3gram and _index_prefix sub-fields for type-ahead; .keyword for exact match.
    PostgreSQL: entities.canonical_name
  • aliasestext .keyword
    Alternative spellings, abbreviations and handles.
    PostgreSQL: entity_aliases.alias WHERE alias_kind IN (name, abbreviation, handle)
  • descriptiontext
    Short description shown in the autocomplete row.
    PostgreSQL: entities.description
  • fact_countinteger
    How many validated facts point at this entity; popularity signal.
    PostgreSQL: count(facts) WHERE object_entity_id = entities.id AND status = validated
  • creator_countinteger
    Distinct creators with a validated fact to this entity; used as a log1p boost in autocomplete.
    PostgreSQL: count(DISTINCT facts.subject_entity_id) WHERE the subject is a creator’s entity (entities.creator_id IS NOT NULL)
  • embeddingknn_vector 1024
    Name + description embedding for did-you-mean and semantic entity lookup. Excluded from _source.
    PostgreSQL: entities.embedding
  • statuskeyword
    active | merged | deleted; only active entities are returned.
    PostgreSQL: entities.status
  • pg_versionlong
    The entity’s change time in microseconds, like the other indexes. A new fact that points at the entity changes fact_count and creator_count but not this version; external_gte accepts an equal version, so the counts still update.
    PostgreSQL: extract(epoch from entities.updated_at) * 1000000
  • updated_atdate
    Row timestamp.
    PostgreSQL: entities.updated_at

Example: hybrid search

Hybrid search = two searches at the same time.

  • Words search: finds creators who use the same words as the question.
  • Meaning search: finds creators who talk about the same thing, even with other words.

OpenSearch gives every creator a mark from each search and mixes the two marks into one ranking.

Design notes

Why the system is built this way, how it copes with 1 million creators, what it costs, what we still need to decide, and short answers to common questions.

Design considerations

What we have to live with

Instagram’s limits

We can make only 200 requests an hour per connected Instagram account. Instagram only shows business and creator accounts, and its photo and video links stop working after a few hours.

Example: to check 54,000 profiles a day we need about 13 connected accounts.

AI is expensive

The AI is our biggest running cost. So we choose carefully which posts it looks at, and which AI model does each job.

Example: a small creator gets one post analysed a month, not every post.

Size

We aim for 1 million creators and about 40 million posts.

More platforms later

TikTok and YouTube must fit in later without rebuilding the system.

Reaching creators

A brand can’t send the first Instagram message, and Instagram doesn’t give us email addresses.

Example: we find hello@studio-vega.example in the creator’s bio and write from Petalwork’s own email address.

Five rules we follow

Each table has one owner

Only one part of the system writes to each table. The others only read it. If something goes wrong, it can only damage its own data.

Example: only Ingest saves posts. Enrich reads them but never changes them.

Each step looks for its own work

Steps don’t send each other messages. Every minute, each step asks the database what the step before it has finished and it hasn’t handled yet.

Example: Enrich asks: “which posts are downloaded but not analysed yet?”

The database is the truth

PostgreSQL holds the real data. S3 keeps the original files, and OpenSearch keeps a copy made for fast search. Both can be rebuilt.

Example: if the search copy breaks, we rebuild it from the database and lose nothing.

Platforms plug in

Everything that is specific to Instagram sits in one piece of code, an “adapter”. A new platform means a new adapter, not a new system.

Example: YouTube gets its own adapter; TikTok comes through a data provider.

AI suggests, rules decide

The AI suggests facts. Rules decide what is kept: the AI must be sure enough, and it must show the proof.

Example: “Studio Vega is in Lisbon, 83% sure”, with the Reel that says “here in my Lisbon studio”. That passes, so it is saved.

Timers and regular checks

Only 7 timers, however many creators

3 planners (Ingest, Enrich, Index) run every minute. Post Tracker runs every 5 minutes. The Experiment Engine, the Relationship Planner and the Watchdog run every hour. With 10 creators or 1 million, it is the same 7 timers.

Each timer looks at a short list

A planner asks “who is due now?” and takes at most 5,000 at a time. With 1 million creators that is about 54,000 checks a day, about 38 a minute.

Example: Post Tracker only looks at posts from the last 30 minutes, not all 40 million.

What really limits us

  • Instagram: about 13 connected accounts, plus a data provider.
  • Claude: about 220,000 tokens a minute (a token is about ¾ of a word).
  • Videos: 40 video workers are enough. About 14 are busy on average, 27 at peak times.

Rules that keep it fast

  1. Every “who is due?” question uses an index, like the index at the back of a book. Without it, each run would read all 40 million posts.
  2. Each run takes only a fixed number of items.
  3. Two runs never take the same creator: the first marks it “busy” for 15 minutes.
  4. Small creators are checked once a month. Every week would need about 20 more Instagram accounts.
  5. We watch how long each run takes, and how long work waits in the queues.
  • At 50 million creators we would change the design: a bigger message system (Kafka or Kinesis), a database split over several servers, and small creators only from data providers.

Questions

Short answers to the questions this design usually raises.

Why not scrape Instagram?

It breaks Instagram’s rules and can be illegal. We only use Instagram’s official API and licensed data providers, so we can always show where our data came from.

What about personal (non-business) accounts?

Instagram’s official API can’t see them. A data provider can give us their public profile, but only a few of their posts.

How fresh is the data?

Big creators are checked every day, small ones once a month. After a brand sends a gift, that creator is checked every day for 30 days. A change shows up in search within 1 to 2 minutes.

How do we know a fact is correct?

Every fact has a confidence score and the proof behind it. Facts the AI is unsure about go to a person. We test the AI on 500 hand-checked facts and want at least 9 in 10 right.

Example: “Studio Vega is in Lisbon” comes with the Reel that says “here in my Lisbon studio”.

What happens when we improve a prompt or switch AI model?

We run the new version again on the original files we kept in S3. The new facts replace the old ones, but nothing is deleted, so we can compare old and new.

Why PostgreSQL and not a graph database?

Most of our work is saving and updating rows safely, and PostgreSQL does that well, in one database. If we later need long chains of links, we can add a graph copy next to it.

Why no events between steps?

Each step finds its work in the tables of the step before it. So each table has one owner, and a lost message or a crash only delays work. The price is up to a minute of extra waiting per step. For data that changes daily or weekly, that is fine.

Why not Step Functions?

AWS Step Functions would track every post through about 8 stages, about 48,000 posts a day, and keep that status in a second place. Its cheaper mode also stops after 5 minutes, and loading a big account takes longer. A planner per step keeps the status in the step’s own rows.

How do we add TikTok or YouTube?

We write a new adapter and add one settings row. Everything after Ingest works the same for every platform.

What if Instagram or Bedrock is down?

The work waits in the database. Workers try again a bit later, so nothing is lost. Search keeps working from OpenSearch in the meantime.

What does it cost to run?

Mostly AI: about 3.5 cents per analysed post, about $55,000 a month for 1 million creators. We keep it down by analysing fewer posts, a cheap check first, bulk prices and spending limits.

How do you reach creators without DMs?

By email. Instagram doesn’t let a brand send the first message and doesn’t give us emails, so we find them in bios, on websites or through a data provider. Emails come from the brand’s own address, with an unsubscribe link and a daily limit. When a creator says stop, we stop at once.

How do you know a sale came from a creator?

Each creator gets a discount code and a link. An order with the code counts first. With no code, the last click on the creator’s link in the past 30 days counts. Codes can leak to coupon sites, and links miss people who switch from phone to laptop; the dashboards say so.

Example: an order with the code VEGA15 counts for Studio Vega.

What about TikTok?

TikTok’s official API gives much less than Instagram’s, so we plan to get TikTok data from a data provider, and check what they may give us before we sign. YouTube has a good official API, so it gets its own adapter later (phase 5).
